## Problem Definition

A steam plant throws away real energy in its condensate. The water leaving a heat exchanger
is already hot, already treated, and already distilled, so the cheapest thing to do with it
is put it back in the boiler. Moving it there is the hard part. An electric pump on that
duty handles liquid near its saturation point, which is the condition centrifugal pumps
cavitate in.

A pressure-powered pump sidesteps that by having no impeller and no motor. It is a vessel
with a float in it:

- Condensate flows in under gravity and the float rises.
- At a set level a spring-loaded linkage goes over centre and **snaps**, throwing the motive
  steam inlet open and the exhaust shut in one motion.
- Steam pressure pushes the charge out against whatever back pressure the plant imposes.
- The level falls, the float drops, the linkage snaps back, and it fills again.

Nothing rotates and nothing cavitates. The trade is that the snap is also where it breaks.

<PumpMechanismFigure />

The linkage turns on a pivot pin, and that pin is the life-limiting part of the pump:

- It is loaded by **impact, not rotation**. The mechanism is built to store spring energy and
  release it discontinuously, so every cycle ends in a strike.
- It sits in **near-boiling condensate** the whole time, so mechanical wear and corrosive wear
  run together rather than separately.
- Wear damages the pump **twice**. The pin thins, the linkage develops slop, and the trip
  points drift until it stops snapping cleanly — and the material removed does not leave.
  Debris stays in the vessel with the mechanism that generated it.

So: which pin material lasts longest in that environment? The honest answer takes years. A
pump in service cycles a few dozen times an hour, the pin is buried inside a pressure vessel,
and you cannot instrument a customer's process to find out. Waiting for the field to answer
is not a test strategy.

## Architecture Overview

The rig is a horizontal shell with a bolted cover plate that carries everything, so the whole
assembly lifts out for inspection without disturbing the tank.

<RigLayoutFigure />

**The tank.**

- Heated salt water instead of condensate, at atmospheric pressure.
- An electric immersion heater holds temperature; float valves hold level.
- Circulation pipes move water between the heater compartment and the mechanism compartments,
  so a specimen is never sitting in a thermal gradient.
- The shell is redox coated — a rig that corrodes faster than the part under test has stopped
  being a measurement.
- **Two mechanism compartments** flanking the heater, so two materials can run side by side
  under identical conditions.

**The drive.**

- A pneumatic cylinder with an extended piston rod, mounted on top of the cover plate.
- At the end of the rod, a slider captured between two guide pins.
- The slider runs in a slot bolted to the float lever nose, swinging the lever through the
  same arc a rising and falling float would give it.
- Air inlet and exhaust valves reproduce the pressure the mechanism normally switches against,
  so the linkage is loaded rather than free-running.

<MechanismMountFigure />

**A run.** Measure the pin, install it, start the rig, let it cycle, stop at the target count,
strip the mechanism, measure again. Wear is the difference between the two measurements,
converted from diameter loss to volume removed.

## Key Design Decisions

**Actuate the mechanism; do not reproduce the process.** The rig never pumps anything — no
condensate, no steam, no discharge line. The slow part of the real cycle is filling a vessel
by gravity, which is exactly the part that has nothing to do with how the pin wears. Delete
it and cycle rate becomes a design variable instead of a constraint.

**Design the schedule before the hardware.** A force balance across the piston, rod, friction,
and the mechanism's own spring load gives a second-order differential equation, solved by
fourth-order Runge–Kutta in MATLAB. It returned 0.914 s for a complete cycle. That assumes the
cylinder's pressure force acts at full value throughout, which it does not, so it was treated
as a floor and buffered to two or three seconds. At that rate a million cycles takes about a
month. The test was known to fit the schedule before any metal was cut.

<CycleDynamicsFigure />

**Drive it with force, not with position.** An over-centre toggle is built to resist motion
until it suddenly does not. A stiff, position-driven coupling has to fight the mechanism
through that transition and hold it at every intermediate angle. A pneumatic cylinder pushes
with a bounded force and lets the mechanism snap on its own stored energy instead of dragging
it across. Surplus force is what buys the accelerated cycle rate.

**Let the slot absorb the geometry.** The cylinder travels in a straight line, the float lever
swings on an arc, and the slider-in-slot reconciles them with one moving part and no extra
joints — leaving the mechanism free to complete its own snap rather than being held to the
actuator's position at every instant.

**Measure wear by volume, not by mass.** The pin is a plain cylinder, so a diameter measured
before and after converts directly to material removed. Mass loss on a part that small sits
inside the noise of anything a student lab can weigh, and profilometry answers a question
about roughness rather than about how much pin is left.

**Accelerate the environment with what is affordable.** Real service is hot condensate under
pressure, and reproducing that faithfully means a boiler and a pressure vessel, neither of
which belongs on a bench with people around it. Heated salt water gets temperature and an
aggressive corrosive load at atmospheric pressure.

**Shortlist against the property set, not a datasheet ranking.** The pin needs wear and galling
resistance, corrosion and stress-corrosion resistance, fatigue strength, and impact toughness,
and those pull against each other. Three candidates reached test — Material 1, Material 2, and
Material 3.

## Tradeoffs

**The rig accelerates cycles, not physics.** Running one cycle every few seconds instead of
every few minutes assumes wear per cycle is independent of how often cycles happen. Dwell under
load and corrosion between strikes both plausibly scale with elapsed time rather than cycle
count, and a rig this fast under-represents them. The acceleration factor is asserted, never
measured.

**Salt water is a proxy, and not conservative in every direction.** Harsher than condensate
chemically, which is the point — but it omits flash steam, the real thermal cycle, and the
water chemistry a treated system actually runs.

**The comparison the rig was built to make never happened.** Two compartments existed
specifically so two materials could run under identical conditions. Technical difficulties meant
testing one at a time, and only Material 1 completed a run. The output is one absolute number
rather than the ranking across three the whole design was aimed at — the least useful form the
result could have taken.

**One specimen, one measurement, no repeat.** No scatter attached, so no way to tell whether the
figure is representative or an outlier.

**The run stopped at half the planned count** — 500,000 cycles rather than a million.

**Before-and-after cannot see the shape of the wear curve.** Two measurements give an average
rate and nothing else. Fast run-in followed by a flat steady state, and steady linear wear
throughout, produce the same endpoint and extrapolate to completely different service lives.
Since extrapolating to service life is the entire purpose, this is the most consequential gap
in the method.

## Results

- **Cycle rate:** two to two-point-eight seconds per cycle against the 0.914 s the differential
  equation predicted — inside the buffer the schedule was built with. A million cycles fits in
  roughly a month at that rate.
- **Wear:** Material 1 completed 500,000 cycles. Wear was measured as diameter loss converted to
  volume removed, and the pin was still serviceable at the end of it.
- **Not run:** Materials 2 and 3 never got a cycle.
- **The most durable result was not an objective.** The rig actuates a float lever through its
  arc in a hot corrosive tank, and nothing about it is specific to the pump it was built for.
  Any snap-action float linkage mounts on the same cover plate and runs on the same schedule.
  It was commissioned as a test for one product; what it actually is is a mechanism reliability
  rig.

## What I'd Improve Next

The binding problem is that the rig was built to compare materials and produced a measurement
of one. In order:

1. **Run the remaining two candidates** on the same rig at the same conditions. One number is a
   data point; three are a decision.
2. **Repeat each material** — enough specimens to put an uncertainty on the figure, because a
   result with no scatter cannot be argued with or trusted.
3. **Measure during the run, not only at the ends.** Periodic teardown, or an in-situ measurement
   of linkage slop, resolves the wear curve instead of its average. That is what determines
   whether the extrapolation to service life is sound.
4. **Calibrate the acceleration factor.** One field-returned pin of known service hours, measured
   by the same volume method, converts the rig from relative comparison to absolute life
   prediction. Cheapest high-value addition on the list.
5. **Get the geometry out of the solver.** The MATLAB carries linkage dimensions as bare numbers
   inside the integration loop and takes run parameters from interactive prompts. Changing the
   linkage means hunting literals, and a run is not reproducible from the file alone.

## Problem Definition

A tapping-mode atomic force microscope reads a surface indirectly. It holds a cantilever
oscillating near resonance, brings the tip to within nanometers of the sample, and infers
the topography from how the oscillation amplitude changes. Nothing touches a ruler. The
measurement is only as good as the relationship between separation and amplitude, so that
relationship is the thing worth simulating.

The cantilever itself is unremarkable: a 41.6 N/m beam at 388.6 kHz with a quality factor
of 300, swinging 20 nm when nothing is near it. The tip–surface force is what makes the
problem hard.

- It is **piecewise**. Above contact only van der Waals attraction acts; below it, elastic
  repulsion takes over.
- It is **strongly nonlinear**: attraction falls off as the inverse square of the gap,
  repulsion rises as the three-halves power of the indentation.
- It turns the system **stiff**. The force changes by orders of magnitude over a few
  ångström of travel while the drive period stays fixed.
- The system is **non-autonomous**, because the drive is an explicit function of time.

<ForceLawFigure />

Which leaves the question the simulation exists to answer: across the separations an AFM
actually operates at, which part of that force shows up in the amplitude signal at all?

## Architecture Overview

The cantilever is reduced to a point mass on a spring, one mode and one degree of freedom,
then non-dimensionalized before anything is solved. Position is scaled by the free
amplitude A<sub>0</sub> = 20 nm and time by 1/ω<sub>0</sub>, which gives a two-state
first-order system:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot{x}_1 &= x_2 \\
\dot{x}_2 &= \lambda F_D(\tau) + \xi F_{ts}(x_1) - \alpha x_2 - \beta x_1
\end{aligned}
$$

with four groups carrying every physical parameter:

$$
\lambda = \frac{1}{m \omega_0^2 A_0}, \qquad
\alpha = \frac{\gamma}{m \omega_0} = \frac{1}{Q}, \qquad
\beta = 1, \qquad
\xi = \lambda
$$

and a drive detuned just below resonance:

$$
F_D(\tau) = F_0 \cos\!\left(\frac{\omega_D}{\omega_0}\, \tau\right)
$$

The scaling has a useful consequence. The drive amplitude works out to

$$
\lambda F_0 = \frac{F_0}{m \omega_0^2 A_0} = \frac{1}{300} = \frac{1}{Q} = \alpha
$$

so the resonant steady-state response is exactly 1. Every amplitude in the results reads
directly as a fraction of free swing, and the analytical check returning 0.9986 instead of
1.0000 is a test of the scaling itself, not just of the solver.

F<sub>ts</sub> is then introduced in three deliberate tiers, each adding exactly one thing:

1. **F<sub>ts</sub> = 0.** Linear, and solvable in closed form. This tier exists only to
   validate the solver.
2. **van der Waals only.** Hamaker constant 6×10⁻²⁰ J, tip radius 20 nm. Nonlinear, but
   the tip never reaches the surface.
3. **Full model.** van der Waals plus Hertzian contact through an effective modulus of
   50 GPa. Piecewise, stiff, and the only tier that represents a real measurement.

The sweep over separation is the outer loop: the solver is called once per tip–surface
separation, and the steady-state amplitude is extracted from each run.

## Key Design Decisions

**Non-dimensionalize before writing any code.** In SI units this system carries a mass of
7×10⁻¹² kg, forces around 3×10⁻⁹ N, and displacements of 10⁻¹⁰ m. A solver's default
absolute tolerance of 10⁻⁶ is larger than every quantity in the problem. It would report
success while resolving nothing. Scaled, the states are order 1 and the tolerances mean
what they say.

**Validate where an answer exists, not where you need one.** With F<sub>ts</sub> = 0 the
system has a closed-form solution, obtained symbolically with `dsolve`. That tier is
useless as physics, since the tip never approaches anything, and it is the only place a
solver can be graded against truth. Six MATLAB solvers were run against it: ode23, ode45,
ode113, ode78, and the stiff pair ode15s and ode23s.

**Score magnitude and phase separately.** A magnitude error alone will lie. Two solutions
can agree at every peak and still be shifted a step apart in time, which shows up as a
large amplitude error that is really a timing error. So the peak of the analytical
steady-state solution was located, the numerical solutions sampled a few steps either
side, and both quantities recorded: the error in value, and the offset in peak time.

<SolverErrorFigure />

**Carry ode78 into the nonlinear runs.** Its peak error of 8.8×10⁻⁴ is roughly fifty times
smaller than ode113 at 4.3×10⁻², and nearly three decades better than ode23s at 6.2×10⁻¹.
The two stiff solvers landing mid-pack is the expected result rather than a surprising one.
With F<sub>ts</sub> = 0 the problem is not stiff, and an implicit method's extra machinery
buys nothing. Phase offsets ran to one or two steps for every solver and did not separate
them, so magnitude decided it.

**Read amplitude only from the steady state.** The transient decays with a time constant
of 2Q = 600, so the 4000-unit run is deliberately long: by τ = 3000 the transient is down
to 0.7%, and the amplitude is taken as the maximum over what remains.

## Tradeoffs

**The output grid is coarse, and it constrains the measurement rather than the
integration.** Solutions are returned on a fixed grid of 1 non-dimensional time unit, which
is about six samples per oscillation. MATLAB steps adaptively underneath that grid, so the
integration itself is unaffected. Everything read *off* the grid inherits it. Amplitude
comes out as a maximum over samples, which only lands close to the true peak because the
steady-state window holds roughly 160 cycles and one of them happens to be sampled near its
crest. Phase is worse: measured as a difference of peak indices, it cannot resolve better
than one sample, which is 16% of a cycle. The finding that phase error was one to two steps
everywhere is partly a statement about the grid.

**The solver was ranked in a regime it was not used in.** ode78 won on a problem that is
smooth, linear, and non-stiff. It was then applied to one that is piecewise and stiff, where
the stiff solvers it beat might well win. What the validation genuinely establishes is that
the *implementation* is correct and that ode78 is the strongest non-stiff option, not that
it is the right solver for contact.

**The sweep was written as duplicated functions.** Each separation in the full model is its
own MATLAB function with the whole parameter block restated inside it. Changing the Hamaker
constant means five identical edits, and nothing enforces that they agree. A single function
taking separation as an argument was the right structure and would have cost less to write
than the copies did.

**The frequency-domain check was dropped.** Comparing amplitude and phase response through
an FFT would have measured phase without the grid quantization above. It was attempted and
abandoned for the time-domain peak method under time pressure. That is the one place where
the validation is thinner than it should be.

## Results

**With van der Waals alone, across separations of 1.0 to 1.6 free amplitudes, the amplitude
does not move.** Variation across the entire sweep stays below 10⁻⁸, and the phase
portraits lie on top of one another. Over this range the attraction is real but not
measurable: it perturbs the trajectory far below anything an instrument would resolve.

**With contact included, the amplitude becomes the signal.** Sweeping 0.4 to 1.2, amplitude
rises with separation, close to linearly, until roughly z<sub>c</sub>* = 1, then flattens
into the same insensitive region the van der Waals sweep found. The phase portraits
separate cleanly at small separations instead of overlapping.

The crossover is not a coincidence of the parameters. z<sub>c</sub>* = 1 is the separation
at which the resting gap equals the free amplitude, the first point at which a freely
swinging tip no longer reaches the surface. Below it the tip strikes every cycle and the
amplitude is set by the collision; above it contact stops and only the attraction remains,
which the first sweep already showed to be negligible.

For this parameter set, then: the tapping-mode signal is contact. The phase portraits stay
elliptical rather than circular throughout, which is the damping and the attractive force
showing up as a departure from simple harmonic motion.

## What I'd Improve Next

The binding constraint is that the solver was never validated in the regime that matters.
In order:

1. **Re-run the comparison in contact.** No analytical solution exists there, so the test
   becomes a step-size refinement study, halving the tolerance until the answer stops
   moving, with the stiff solvers back in the running.
2. **Resolve the tap.** The current output grid cannot see a single collision. Peak contact
   force, contact duration, and energy dissipated per cycle are all invisible at six samples
   per period. That needs event detection at the surface crossing and dense output through
   the strike.
3. **Make the system autonomous.** Adding a third state for the drive phase removes the
   explicit time dependence, which simplifies the right-hand side and lets a wider class of
   methods apply cleanly.
4. **Parameterize the sweep** so separation is an argument rather than a copy.
5. **Validate against measured amplitude–distance curves.** Everything above establishes
   internal consistency. Nothing yet establishes that the model matches an instrument.
